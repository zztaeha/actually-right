# Process, taste and evaluation

How to think about a design problem, how to critique it, and how to check it.

## Taste is trainable

**The single most useful finding here: taste is not talent.** The ability to develop taste is **trained through repetitions**, and the method is consistent across sources:

1. **Curate inputs deliberately.** Creative directors curate on purpose and **study the best version of something, not the popular version.**
2. **Build a reference library.** Every later judgment gets checked against it.
3. **Break down what works.** When something catches your eye, ask *why* – typography, layout, color, spacing, motion – and compare it against other examples.
4. **Collect, live with it, prune.** **The pruning is the part that develops judgment.**
5. **Articulate it.** Design critiques are training grounds where you learn to articulate taste. **Being forced to defend a decision is what converts intuition into knowledge.**

**A skill that can *name* why something works is doing the thing that builds taste** – for itself and for the user. `brand-corpus.md` is the reference library; the "reads as" vocabulary is the naming.

## Divergence before convergence

- **Divergent phase: quantity, deliberately including bold and unconventional options**, explicitly to prevent limiting yourself to one or two viable solutions too early.
- **Convergent phase: rank, sort, combine, select.**
- **Divergence measurably increases both novelty and quality** of the final design – not just novelty.

**This is the theoretical grounding for the three-variant protocol.** The variants are not a presentation device, **they are the divergent phase made concrete**, and the research says skipping it produces worse work, not just less interesting work.

**The corollary matters as much: the three variants must be genuinely different directions, not three color schemes of one layout.** Three near-identical options is **convergence disguised as divergence**, and it forfeits the entire benefit.

## The discovery questions that earn their place

- **What does the business need this to achieve?** Not "what do you want" – what outcome.
- **Who is this for, and what do they already believe** when they arrive?
- **Show me work you admire.** And crucially: **show me work you think is bad, and say why.** **The negative examples are more informative than the positive ones** – they reveal the actual boundaries.
- **Is there an existing brand or design guideline?**
- Brand personality by indirection: **if your brand were a newspaper, which one, and why?** Clients who cannot speak design language can answer this.
- **What is the constraint I do not know about yet?** Framework, timeline, legal, a stakeholder with a veto.
- **Where does traffic actually come from** – mobile or desktop?
- **Font choice.** Always asked, never assumed.

> **Ask questions whose answers change what you build. A question with only one plausible answer is a wasted turn.**

## Starting from a blank page

- **Design projects begin with a question, not a sketch.** Start with the problem, not the canvas.
- **Mind map first, on paper.** Mind mapping and sketching help process ideas, **and this does not happen the same way if the process starts immediately on a screen.**
- **Generate quickly and without judgment.** The point of the first pages is to **get every bad idea out** to make room for the good ones.
- **A blank page feels infinite and overwhelming; an outline and a wireframe limit possibilities, which makes starting feel doable. The constraint is what unblocks.**

**The right first move on an open brief is not to start generating a layout.** It is to **establish the problem, impose a constraint set** – direction, palette, type, motion character – **and only then build.** That order is the entire difference between designing and producing.

## Constraints produce better work

The relationship between constraint and creativity is **curvilinear:** a certain level of constrainedness is a **prerequisite** to creativity. Not zero, not maximum.

- **When there are no constraints, complacency sets in and people follow the path of least resistance. Unlimited freedom produces the *average* solution** – precisely the mechanism behind AI slop.
- Constraints direct cognitive focus, reduce overload, and force connections between unrelated sources.
- Measured effects: **more radical creativity, more adaptation and activity, less cognitive overload, less feature fatigue, less topic drift.**
- **Constraints transform vague aspirations into actionable challenges.**

**This justifies imposing constraints rather than offering unlimited options.** A spec that says *three colors, two typefaces, one motion character, an 8 pt grid* is not a limitation on the design – **it is the thing that makes the design possible.**

## Norman's three levels

| Level | What it is | Governed by |
|---|---|---|
| **Visceral** | Immediate, pre-conscious reaction to appearance. Largely outside the user's control | Color, type, imagery, composition, craft. **Fires in ~0.05 s** |
| **Behavioral** | Usability, function, performance, effectiveness in use | Hierarchy, states, feedback latency, error handling, responsive behavior |
| **Reflective** | Meaning, self-image, the story told afterward, whether they recommend it | Brand, voice, values, the memory of the experience |

**A good design addresses all three, and they fail independently.** A beautiful product that is hard to use fails behaviorally. **A usable product with no character fails reflectively – which is exactly the AI-slop failure: behaviorally fine, visceral neutral, reflectively forgettable.**

**The cleanest framework for why craft is not decoration. The visceral level is not the shallow one – it is the one that fires first, sets trust, and colors everything after it.**

## Nielsen's 10 heuristics – the evaluation spine

**Unchanged since 1994**, still the most widely applied evaluation framework in practice.

1. **Visibility of system status** – feedback latency, notifications, save state.
2. **Match between system and the real world** – speak the user's language.
3. **User control and freedom** – undo, escape hatches, no scrolljacking.
4. **Consistency and standards** – conventions over invention.
5. **Error prevention** – better than error messages. Validation timing, constraints stated up front.
6. **Recognition rather than recall** – labels over memorized icons, visible shortcuts.
7. **Flexibility and efficiency of use** – accelerators for experts without penalizing novices.
8. **Aesthetic and minimalist design** – every element competes with the important ones.
9. **Help users recognize, diagnose and recover from errors** – what / why / next.
10. **Help and documentation** – findable, task-focused.

> **Their strength is their generality:** they do not say "put the login button top right", they say speak the user's language, give immediate feedback, be consistent.

**The right backbone for a diagnostic pass. They name what class of thing is wrong, and the rest of this skill supplies the specific fix. A heuristic without a fix is a complaint; a fix without a heuristic is a preference.**

## Cognitive load

- **Miller: ~7 items held at once.** Beyond that, tasks get harder, details are missed, and people feel overwhelmed. The source of the 7±2 rules in settings and navigation.
- **Hick: decision time grows with the number and complexity of choices.**
- Three practical levers: **chunking**, **progressive disclosure**, and **visual hierarchy** so the user can prioritize without reading everything.

**Together they are why a dense screen with good grouping outperforms a sparse screen with none. Load is about structure, not quantity.**

## Delight – the honest version

- **The paradox:** a good user experience flows so effortlessly it does not stand out in memory. And alongside it: **world-class UX should be frictionlessly forgettable, but defining brand moments should be delightfully indelible.**
- **The resolution: most of the product should be invisible; a small number of moments should be unmistakable.** Spreading delight evenly produces neither.
- **Repetition is what turns delight into friction.** When animations are experienced dozens of times per day, **any delay is perceived as friction.**
- Reported bounds: click-triggered micro-interactions **under ~500 ms**; hover-driven ambient animation can run longer (1500–3000 ms) because it is not blocking anything.
- **Delight is subjective.** What charms one person irritates another, and **novelty for its own sake reliably annoys.**
- Emotion is qualitative; it needs qualitative methods to find, not analytics.

## Rams, and why not to quote him uncritically

The ten principles translate unusually well to software, and **"less, but better"** is a genuinely useful corrective. **But the critiques are real:**

- **They presume you know in advance how the design will be used.** Digital products get used in ways nobody designed for; that assumption is weaker in software than in a radio.
- **It is a specifically Western, 20th-century minimalism.** To some designers Rams' products are sleek and elegant; to others they appear bland and forgettable. A 2025 paper in *Design and Culture* explicitly calls to move beyond the ten principles toward plural, evolving definitions of good design.
- **Applied dogmatically, they produce exactly the restrained, tasteful, unmemorable work that the AI-slop critique also lands on** – arriving at sameness from the opposite direction.

**Use Rams as one available position, named as such.** Excellent for tools, instruments, dashboards and anything instrumental; **actively wrong for brands whose job is warmth, joy, spectacle or cultural specificity. Be able to argue *for* maximalism when the brief calls for it, and know that this is not a failure of discipline.**

## Critique

**Giving:**

- **Ask whether feedback is wanted before giving it.**
- **Match the feedback to the stage.** "Change direction entirely" is useful in ideation and destructive at the end. **Say what stage you are in and what kind of feedback you want.**
- **Be specific and cite the actual element.** "I like it" is not feedback; "the eyebrow spacing sets up the heading well" is.
- Critique the work, never the person.

**Receiving:**

- Take notes; ask clarifying questions; **repeat back what you heard** to confirm you understood.
- **Ask for a specific example when feedback is vague.**
- **Close the loop:** say what you addressed, what you did not, and why. **The step almost everyone skips, and what makes the next critique better.**

**State up front what kind of feedback you want at each stage** – "this is a direction check, not a pixel review" – **and close the loop when feedback arrives**, saying what changed and what deliberately did not.

### The self-critique problem

**A self-graded checklist is the weakest possible form of critique** – the same mind that made the decision grading it, with no external reference. Better mechanisms:

- **Check against the reference library rather than against a rule.** "Does this hold up next to the best example of this kind of thing?" is harder and more useful than "does this pass the checklist?"
- **Make it verifiable.** Contrast ratios, 320 px reflow, grayscale hierarchy, tab order – these can be **run**, not asserted.
- **Name the trade-off you made and its cost.** A design decision with no stated cost has not been examined.
- **State what you would change with more time.** This forces an honest ranking of the remaining weaknesses.

## Presenting design work

- **Start with the problem, the user and the goal.** Give stakeholders a clear lens for evaluating before showing anything. **Without a lens, every review becomes a preference argument.**
- **Show that alternatives were considered and say why this one won.** Presenting one option reads as an assertion; **presenting the rejected paths reads as a decision.**
- **Frame around "why", not "what".** This shifts the mood of the meeting from judgment to collaboration.
- **Direct attention deliberately** and say what kind of feedback you want.
- Connect to business goals and use language the audience already has.

**This is exactly the three-variant interaction: three genuinely different directions with the reasoning attached, the trade-off each one makes named, and a recommendation. That is a design presentation, not a menu.**

### Design rationale

**A design rationale forces designers to be explicit about what they are doing and why.** It records the **reasoning, constraints and trade-offs** behind decisions.

**Ship rationale with the design.** Not a paragraph of justification – **the specific decision, the alternative rejected, and the cost accepted. Three lines per significant decision.** That is what makes a design reviewable rather than merely likeable, and **it is the difference between a partner and a generator.**

## Testing – the cheap version that actually happens

- **Five users find ~85% of usability issues.** Krug argues three is enough. **Quantity is less of the concern – regular testing is.**
- **Guerrilla testing:** one clear goal, two or three simple tasks, **5–10 minute sessions**, real people in a public setting. Insights within hours. Best in early design stages.
- Automated accessibility tooling catches **30–40%** – the same lesson: **cheap and frequent beats thorough and rare.**

**You cannot run a usability test from here, but you can name the three tasks worth testing** and say that **five people and an afternoon will answer the question better than another round of opinion. Knowing when to stop designing and go ask someone is part of the judgment.**

### The five-second test

- **Opinion forms in ~50 ms; a decision to stay takes ~5–7 seconds.**
- Method: show the design briefly, then ask what they remember and what they think it is for.
- **What it exposes:** gaps in clarity, messaging problems, unclear hierarchy, and elements that get overlooked entirely.
- **15–20 participants** for statistical usefulness – more than the 5 needed for usability issues, **because first impressions vary more.**

**The human version of the squint test.** Run the squint test yourself; **name the five-second test as the thing to do next** when the question is "does this communicate", because that is a question no amount of self-review answers.

## Design QA

Design QA asks **does it look and feel right**, where traditional QA asks **does it work**.

- **Visual alignment and spacing** – across all breakpoints, not just the design width.
- **Typography hierarchy** – sizes and weights actually resolving to the scale.
- **Color contrast** – measured, not eyeballed.
- **Interaction states** – all of them, on every interactive element.
- **Brand consistency** – tokens used, no inline values.
- **Spacing between paragraphs, images and interactive elements** – specifically where consistency shows.

**And the process point that matters more than the list: QA belongs throughout, not at the end.** Checkpoints after wireframe, during visual design, before handoff. **A polish pass at the end cannot fix a structural problem.**

### Verification tooling

- **Responsive:** DevTools device emulation for the loop; **Responsively App** to see many viewports simultaneously; **real devices before launch.** Emulators cannot reproduce touch, real network, real GPU or real font rendering.
- **Visual regression:** Percy, Chromatic, Applitools, **Playwright's built-in visual comparison (free)**, BackstopJS. The 2026 shift is **from pixel diffing to semantic comparison**, which cuts the false-positive noise that killed adoption of pixel-based tooling.
- **Contrast:** APCA calculator, `apca-w3`, Bridge-PCA.
- **Accessibility:** axe and Lighthouse as a floor (**30–40% coverage**), then manual keyboard, zoom and screen reader passes.

## Content first, and real content only

- **Lorem ipsum divorces layout from meaning.** Designers create layouts using placeholder text that look beautiful in presentations, **but real content does not cooperate.**
- **Real headlines are longer and real copy is messier than placeholder.** Retrofitting real content into a layout designed around fake content causes late-stage rework.
- **Content-first costs more early collaboration and usually shortens total time-to-launch**, because the big disconnects never happen.
- With placeholder text, clients and users cannot see much past the layout – **so reviews become about aesthetics rather than communication.**

**Design with real data, including the ugly cases:** extremely long names, names with diacritics and non-Latin scripts, single-character names, missing values, zero states, enormous numbers, negative numbers, very old and very future dates.

**Analyze logs and real records to find the actual edge cases** rather than inventing them.

> **Every demo should use plausible real content and include at least one deliberately awkward record** – the longest name, the empty field, the six-figure number. **A design that only works with the tidy example is not finished, and building with tidy examples is exactly how AI-generated UI ends up brittle.**

## Redesigns

- **A case study that does not document the baseline cannot make a meaningful claim about what changed.** Capture traffic, conversion, bounce and revenue-per-visit **before touching anything.**
- **The strongest redesigns do not rely on one dramatic fix. They improve a series of smaller decisions that remove hesitation and make the next step obvious.**
- Metrics that matter: bounce rate, conversion rate, page load speed, session duration, revenue impact.

**When asked to redesign, ask what the current numbers are first.** If the user does not have them, **say that the redesign will be unmeasurable and offer to capture a baseline.** That is what a senior person does before touching a working page.

## Brand vs conversion

**The CTA color that converts best might not match your brand palette; the form layout that lifts leads might go against your design system.**

- **Brand** builds loyalty, emotional connection and differentiation – slow, compounding, hard to measure.
- **CRO** is immediate and quantifiable – **and optimizes toward local maxima, which is how everything converges on the same page.**
- **The resolution: start CRO with UX optimization, because it is least likely to conflict with the brand.** Clarity, speed, hierarchy, error handling and friction removal lift conversion **without touching identity.** Only when those are exhausted does the trade become real.

**When asked to "make it convert better", the first pass is UX debt, not brand compromise – and say so.**

### Onboarding checklists

- **3–5 tasks** leading to the activation moment.
- **The endowed progress effect: pre-check item one.** Users who see "1 of 5 complete" are more likely to continue than users who see "0 of 5". **Free, and one of the most reliably replicated effects in this area.**
- **Zeigarnik effect:** a visible progress bar creates the pull to finish an incomplete task.
- Reported: gamified checklists drive **~50% higher completion**; one platform saw **+40% activation**. Vendor-published, directional only.
- **The caution: gamification backfires in B2B when it feels condescending.** Progress and clarity travel well; badges, streaks and confetti do not always.

## Cross-cultural design

Localization is **not simply translating interfaces – it is a deep dive into the cultural fabric that shapes behaviors, expectations and values.**

- **The hamburger icon is not universal** – it is misunderstood in markets with different UX conventions.
- **White space reads differently.** Whitespace, an essential staple of good design in the West, **may be seen as a waste of space in the East.** **Dense East-Asian commerce interfaces are not badly designed; they are correctly designed for a different expectation.** The most important correction in this area, because Western designers routinely read density as failure.
- **Color symbolism does not transfer.**
- Individualist cultures respond to personalization and autonomy; collectivist cultures to community and shared experience.
- Local competitor analysis and testing with local users are the practical methods.

## Inclusive imagery

- **Take a portfolio view.** It is unrealistic to expect a single photo to show the diversity of your audience. **Coherence and representation are both properties of the *set*.**
- **Vary multiple characteristics independently.** When you show Black people, do not always show the same skin tone, hair texture and body size. **Repeating one combination is a stereotype with extra steps.**
- **Show marginalized people in active, strong roles**, not defaulting to those who historically held power.
- **The question to ask: who is missing or excluded?** Then check whether that absence is a pattern.
- **When people do not see themselves in images, they feel invisible – and may even perform worse on tasks.**

## White-label and multi-brand

**One shared codebase, many differentiated behaviors.** Theming is a **token swap** if the semantic layer is right.

**The thing most systems get wrong: theming only color and type.** A brand's identity also lives in **corner radius, outline style, shadow use, spacing density and motion character.** **A multi-brand system that varies only color produces brands that look like the same product wearing different paint.**

Layering: one set of base components matched to the primary brand, then additional decision layers for sub-brands.

The honest note: **building a genuinely flexible white-label product is one of the more challenging design problems** – the flexibility fights the coherence, always.

## An honest read on AI design tools

Worth holding, because this skill is one.

**What they do well:** fast exploration, polished output from short prompts, plausible states, near-instant iteration, and **covering the boring surface area – empty and error states, variants – that gets skipped by hand.**

**What they do badly, per practitioners:**

- **Generated output ignores your design system until you clean it.**
- **No structural knowledge** – it can show the wall removed without knowing whether it was load-bearing.
- **Colors are approximations, not guarantees.**
- **Typography generation lacks the precision of real typesetting.**
- And the systemic one: **they converge on the statistical average.**

**The most effective setup is not one AI tool at all – it is a small stack of specialized tools connected to the workflow the team already uses.**

### Which makes this the brief for this skill

1. **Work inside the user's design system**, not around it. **Read the tokens that exist before proposing new ones.**
2. **Commit to a direction** rather than averaging.
3. **Verify rather than assert** – contrast measured, 320 px checked, tab order walked, squint test run.
4. **Say what was traded.**
5. **Cover the boring surface area properly** – all five states, every breakpoint, the error copy – **because that is genuinely where machine thoroughness beats human patience.**
